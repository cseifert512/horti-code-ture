# Campsis × tagliabueana 'Madame Galen'

**Common name:** Trumpet creeper 'Madame Galen' (trumpet vine)  
**Place in palette:** OPTION — warm/sheltered late-summer display

A hybrid of the American *Campsis radicans* and the Chinese *C. grandiflora*, put into commerce in 1889 and "the finest and best known" of the forms distributed by French nurseries (Trees and Shrubs Online, which spells the cultivar 'Mme Galen'). Much of what floras record is recorded of the two parents, not of the cultivar. Where a statement below rests on a parent, it says so. Quotations were checked against the pages on 13 November 2026; where a page could not be checked word for word, its content is given in other words and not in quotation marks.

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Large, strong-growing deciduous woody climber with a stout main stem (RHS record; Bean of *C. radicans*: "a stout main stem"). |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Aerial rootlets, but a weak self-clinger: Trees and Shrubs Online says of 'Mme Galen' "It is not a climber that can be left to look after itself" and that "the growths of young plants should be carefully spaced and tied in". Plan for ties as well as a clingable surface. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 8–12 m tall, 2.5–4 m across; 5–10 years to ultimate class. |
| Hardiness | H4 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Late into leaf. Salmon-red trumpets in loose terminal trusses in late summer and early autumn (RHS: "late summer or early autumn"). Pods rare in Britain. Winter leafless. |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun, south or west facing, sheltered (RHS record); heat strongly affects flowering. See "Light, aspect and shelter". |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained fertile site. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates.

## Species-specific biological logic

Builds a long-lived woody framework that carries the year's shoots; do not use old-wood wisteria bud-induction rules. It flowers on the current season's growth, in a truss that ends the shoot (RHS pruning group 12: spur-prune the side shoots in late winter).

## Form: what the model draws

Every shape number behind the drawing is in [`morphology.json`](morphology.json) with its source and evidence status; every growth number is in [`model.json`](model.json). This section says in words what the two files and the viewer module (`packages/viewer/src/plant/taxa/campsis-x-tagliabueana-madame-galen.ts`) add up to, and where they simplify the plant. The look was compared with photographs of the cultivar, of the hybrid and of the parent *C. radicans* from Wikimedia Commons (the list is in the report that went with this work; a photograph is named below as "reference <name>").

**Through the year.** In winter the plant is a bare framework: main stems in pale tan bark lying along the timber, last summer's shoots standing out from them as straw-coloured sticks, a few carrying a dry tan pod with the black star of its calyx at the top. It is late into leaf: buds break in the second half of April and the first leaves are out in mid May, so that at the end of May the frame is still mostly bare. Through June and July each framework stem throws shoots of the year, 60 to 80 cm long, that stand out from the support, lean and arch over under their leaves, and carry pairs of pinnate leaves; by late July the frame is clothed. On the trellis the leaves turn out from it and hang face out, the end leaflet lowest, so that a covered face reads as overlapping chevrons (reference hyb-badduben-1). From the end of July a truss forms at the tip of about six shoots in ten. Trusses open in succession until early October and the last are over by the end of that month, so that through August and September some dozens are in flower at once on a plant that fills the specimen frame. In late October the leaves turn a dull yellow-green and they are down by the end of November.

**Leaf.** Opposite, odd-pinnate, 20 to 35 cm long: a short stalk (a tenth of the leaf), an axis, and 7, 9 or 11 leaflets (9 is drawn most often, 7 on short leaves) in opposite pairs with one at the end. A leaflet is ovate, up to 8 by 4.5 cm, widest a third of the way up, broad and rounded at the base, drawn out into a long point, and coarsely toothed with four to seven forward-pointing teeth to a side, uneven in size and spacing, none on the base or the point; the side leaflets lean forward 22 to 34 degrees from square. Each leaflet of a leaf has its own teeth and its own size. The blade is puckered between sunk veins. Mid to dark green and somewhat shiny above, darker in shade; bronze-tinged as it unfolds; dull yellow-green before it falls.

**Truss and flower.** A loose terminal panicle of 6 to 12 flowers: a bare stalk, then the flowers crowded on its last part in opposite branches, the lower carrying two or three flowers and the upper one, so that the truss reads as a head of trumpets facing out among buds. It carries on the line of its shoot and nods over; as the shoot arches, the truss hangs out from it. A flower is a calyx about 2.5 cm long, yellow-green going ochre and orange at its five pointed teeth, and a trumpet up to 8 cm long: a tube narrow and yellowish where it stands in the calyx, orange above, widening to a throat 3 cm across, and a limb 6.5 to 8 cm across of five broad rounded overlapping lobes, the two upper a little smaller. The lobes are a deep salmon-red; the throat is apricot-yellow with fine red lines. Four stamens, two long and two short, with yellow anthers, lie under the upper lobes with the style between them, drawn on the nearest model. The flowers open a few at a time from the base of the truss. A flower in bud is a club standing out of its calyx, orange reddening at its tip; it swells and spreads into the trumpet; the corolla then drops and leaves the calyx. The youngest flowers, at the tip, stay in bud to the end.

**Pod.** A stalked, spindle-shaped capsule 8 to 13 cm long and about 2 cm through, beaked, with a ridge along each of its two seams and fainter ones between, and the dried calyx standing as a dark star at its top (reference hyb-fruit-1); grey-green, drying to pale tan in late autumn, and hanging through the winter from the bare stalk of its truss with the stubs of the fallen flowers. One truss in twenty-five is given one, because pods are rare in Britain.

**Hold.** Tufts of short buff rootlets in two rows, beside and below a node, on the side of a stem that lies against timber. They are drawn only where a framework stem holds a member by itself, and are hidden under the stem once it has thickened.

**Bark.** A young shoot green, flushed red-brown in the sun; straw-coloured by its first winter; then light tan bark in long strips.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- All the year's free growth is drawn as flowering side shoots of one season. The plant also makes long non-flowering growths, which are left out because the engine would keep each one for ever; and on an unpruned plant a shoot of the year stays as wood and branches the next year, which the engine cannot do, so the shoots are shed before each spring as a late-winter spur prune would remove them. The prune itself is not modelled (see "Care: what the gardener does").
- A shoot of the year sets in the shape it has when it stops growing, in late July; the truss that opens at its end afterwards does not bend it further. On the plant the weight of the truss brings many shoots over and down; in the model those near the top of the frame still stand up and out.
- Every leaf in a run is drawn from one range of lengths, so the smaller leaves at the base and tip of a shoot are not smaller.
- The whole framework stops lengthening when the flowering shoots do, in late July. Real extension growth goes on into September.
- A truss opens from its base and its corollas drop in the same order; how many flowers are open at once follows from that and is more than on the plant, where two to five are usual. The bare axis of a spent truss, with its calyces, is drawn until the last corolla has gone and then not at all.
- A pod does not split. On the plant the two valves part in winter and the winged seeds blow out.
- Fruit is set in the same small share every year. On the plant it is none in most British years and some after a hot summer.
- The leaf axis and the young shoot can be flushed red-purple on the plant (reference mg-stang-1); the leaf shader colours the axis from the leaf's greens, so it is drawn green.
- The rootlets say nothing about whether the hold is strong enough. The profile's caveat stands: tie it in.

## Light, aspect and shelter

What the sources say, kept apart by what it is about. The light model shades and nothing else; warmth and shelter are reported here and are not modelled.

**Classes of light it accepts** (as the RHS defines them).

- *Full sun: yes, and it is what the plant is grown in.* RHS plant record: "Full sun". RHS growing guide: campsis needs "full sun in order to ripen the wood so it flowers freely" and "It flowers best in full sun, in a warm sheltered spot". Trees and Shrubs Online, of 'Mme Galen': "not only does it need the sunniest wall but also plenty of room".
- *Partial shade: it grows, and flowers less.* Havlis nursery catalogue, of this cultivar: "It will grow in partial shade, but the flowers will be fewer and their colour less intense." Burncoose Nurseries says it prefers full sun and tolerates some shade in warmer areas, and in colder areas should be grown against a warm, sunny wall. NC State Extension lists full sun or partial shade, for a climate with far hotter summers than Britain's. No British source offers partial shade for this plant.
- *Full shade: no source accepts it.*

**What the recommended aspect is for.** The RHS record gives "South–facing or West–facing" and "Sheltered". The reasons the sources give are warmth first and light second: full sun is asked for "in order to ripen the wood so it flowers freely" (RHS guide), and campsis "needs plenty of space and warmth" (RHS guide: "It needs plenty of space and warmth, so grow it against a large wall or over a sturdy pergola in full sun"). In cooler temperate regions it needs the shelter of a sunny wall to flower abundantly (RHS A–Z Encyclopedia, as cited by Wikipedia; paraphrased). Bean says of *C. radicans* that pods form "Very rarely, but sometimes after unusually hot summers". So a south or west wall is asked for its heat, the sun it gets being part of how it gives that heat.

**Shelter.** From cold wind: the RHS guide says "Although hardy, campsis needs shelter from cold winds". From frost: late frosts can damage young growth (NC State), and Burncoose warns of lost foliage or stem dieback in harsh winters in cold places (paraphrased). From wind and snow on the year's growth: "Trumpet creeper has fragile wood which, if too long, can easily become a target for strong wind or heavy snowfall" (Havlis), and Havlis does not recommend it for "very exposed, windy sites".

**What shade does.** To growth: nothing read says that shade slows it; the sources that mention shade speak only of flowers. To flowering: fewer flowers of less intense colour in partial shade (Havlis), and by the RHS's account unripened wood that does not flower freely.

**What too much sun or exposure does.** No source read reports scorch or any harm from sun. The harm reported is from cold and from drought at the root: "low temperatures or dry soil can cause flower buds to be shed" (RHS guide), although "Campsis is fairly drought tolerant" once established (RHS guide).

**For the model.** The light model reads the profile's sentence as accepting full sun only, which agrees with the British sources. That sentence ties flowering to summer heat, not to light, so the model does not reduce flowering in shade beyond what the engine does to all flowers at the ends of side shoots (scaled by the light the plant has); the sources above would support doing more. Warmth is the stronger requirement and is not modelled at all: a run on a cool, exposed or north-facing structure will flower as freely as one on a south wall, and should not be believed on that point.

## Care: what the gardener does

The gardened plant is the baseline (PROJECT_CONTEXT.md section 3.4). This plant's routine, from the RHS (pruning group 12) and the growers:

- **At planting (March).** "After planting, cut back all the stems to 15cm (6in) from the ground to stimulate new growth." Then "Train the strongest of the resulting new shoots to fill the space" and "Always remove weaker shoots" (RHS growing guide). The model plants a cane of 15 cm (`architecture.nurseryCaneM`) and grows three stems from the base.
- **Years 1 to 3: tying in.** "It takes two or three years for a plant to establish a strong woody framework of branches" (RHS); "the growths of young plants should be carefully spaced and tied in" (Trees and Shrubs Online). The model's trained plant is tied to the timber (`tieToTimber`).
- **Every year once the framework is made, in late winter (February to early March, after hard frost).** "Once a framework is established, usually in two to three years, 'spur-prune' the sideshoots annually in late winter. This involves cutting back all the sideshoots to within two or three buds of the main stems." Also "Remove any weak growth and cut back damaged stems close to their base" (RHS). Havlis puts the same cut "in spring after all frosts have passed" and says "Retain the strong branches, as these will bear the summer inflorescences"; Bean, of *C. radicans*, that it "should be pruned back every spring". Burncoose (its pruning group 8) spur-prunes to three or four buds from the framework.
- **Renovation, when overgrown.** "Cut back all the shoots to within 30cm (12in) of the base in late winter"; "Hard pruning will reduce flowering" (RHS). Not part of the routine.
- **Summer.** No source asks for a summer cut. Long free growths may be tied in or removed where they are in the way (RHS: "Plants can be pruned annually to keep them within bounds").

**What the model does today.** No routine is modelled for this plant (`MODELLED_ROUTINE` in `packages/core/src/sim/planting.ts` does not list it). The shoots of the year are shed before each spring by the growth model itself (`sideShoots.lifespanYears` 1), which is what the late-winter spur prune would leave, without the stubs. As events the engine already has, the routine is: from the third year after planting, on 25 February, `shorten_side_shoots` with `keepNodes` [1, 2] (two or three buds of opposite pairs are one or two nodes; `pruning.winterBudsKept` holds the RHS's two to three buds), `which: 'all'`, `freeShoots: true`, `remains: 'stub'`; with `sideShoots.lifespanYears` then raised so that the stub, not the growth model, decides what is left. That change has not been made or run.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Strong substantial framework, prepared root-adhesion surface or explicit tie points; large open routes, not presumed self-adherent on a thin smooth metal wire. The RHS asks for a large wall or a sturdy pergola. The year's shoots stand 60 to 80 cm clear of the framework and need that room.

## Global structure placement logic

Sunny warm external face/edge, as a stronger late-summer flower alternative; poor match for cool exposed high canopy.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Warm-season colour complement but can become a large competitor for wisteria and evergreens.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

H4 and heat preference reduce portability across UK climates; strong growth/woody mass, flower performance in cool summers uncertain. Suckers from the base and from running roots (Oates et al. 2014; White Flower Farm). Sap may irritate skin.

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

None of the growth numbers has been measured on a plant. The measurements that would settle the most:

- Shoots of the year per metre of framework on a spur-pruned plant, their length, and the share that end in a truss, in a British summer.
- How far a shoot of the year stands out before it arches, and how far it droops under a truss; the diameter of a shoot of the year at its foot; the stiffness of the year's wood; the fresh mass of a leaf and of a truss.
- Internode length on flowering shoots and on extension growth; leaf length along a shoot.
- Dates of budburst, first open flower, last open flower and leaf fall at a British site, over several years.
- Flowers to a truss, how many are open at once, days from first flower to last on one truss.
- Length and number of rootlets, how many nodes bear them, and what pull a rooted stem resists on the timber finish Bower uses: the contact/failed-contact test.
- Stem diameter at the base against age.
- Heat and insolation response of flowering; frequency of pods.

At minimum, bind all measured parameters to plant **age**, plant **part**, time step, site/climate, support properties and propagation/stock history. Keep missing values empty rather than extrapolating from species names or RHS maturity classes.
