# Campsis × tagliabueana 'Madame Galen'

**Common name:** Trumpet creeper 'Madame Galen' (trumpet vine)  
**Place in palette:** OPTION — warm/sheltered late-summer display

A hybrid of the American *Campsis radicans* and the Chinese *C. grandiflora*, put into commerce in France in 1889 and the best known of its kind (Trees and Shrubs Online, which spells the cultivar 'Mme Galen'). Much of what floras record is recorded of the two parents, not of the cultivar. Where a statement below rests on a parent, it says so.

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Large vigorous deciduous woody climber with a stout main stem. |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Aerial rootlets, but a weak self-clinger: the RHS advises tying in, and Bean says this hybrid cannot be left to look after itself. Plan for ties as well as a clingable surface. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 8–12 m tall, 2.5–4 m across; 5–10 years to ultimate class. |
| Hardiness | H4 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Late into leaf. Salmon-red trumpets in loose terminal trusses from late summer until the first frosts. Pods rare in Britain. Winter leafless. |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun, preferably south/west, warm **sheltered** position; heat strongly affects flowering reliability. See "Light, aspect and shelter". |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained fertile site. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates.

## Species-specific biological logic

Builds a long-lived woody framework that carries the year's shoots; do not use old-wood wisteria bud-induction rules. It flowers on the current season's growth, in a truss that ends the shoot (RHS pruning group 12: spur-prune side shoots to two or three buds in late winter).

## Form: what the model draws

Every shape number behind the drawing is in [`morphology.json`](morphology.json) with its source and evidence status; every growth number is in [`model.json`](model.json). This section says in words what the two files and the viewer module (`packages/viewer/src/plant/taxa/campsis-x-tagliabueana-madame-galen.ts`) add up to, and where they simplify the plant.

**Through the year.** In winter the plant is a bare framework: main stems that come to several centimetres through, in light tan bark that peels in thin strips, lying along the timber, with last summer's shoots standing out from them as straw-coloured sticks, a few of them carrying a dry tan pod. It is late into leaf: buds break in the second half of April and the first leaves are out in mid May. Through June and July each framework stem throws shoots of the year, 60 to 80 cm long, that stand clear of the support and carry pairs of large pinnate leaves; by late July the frame is clothed. From the end of July a truss forms at the tip of about six shoots in ten. Trusses open in succession until early October and the last are over by the end of that month, so that through August and September some dozens are in flower at once on a plant that fills the specimen frame. In late October the leaves turn a dull yellow-green and they are down by the end of November.

**Leaf.** Opposite, odd-pinnate, 20 to 35 cm long: a stalk, an arching axis grooved above, and 7, 9 or 11 leaflets (9 is drawn most often, 7 on short leaves) in opposite pairs with one at the end. A leaflet is ovate, up to 8 by 4.5 cm, rounded at the base, drawn out into a long point, and coarsely toothed with the teeth pointing forward; a vein runs to each tooth and is sunk in the blade. Dark green and somewhat shiny above, paler and downy on the veins beneath.

**Truss and flower.** A lax terminal panicle of 6 to 12 flowers, its branches in opposite pairs, the lower ones carrying two or three flowers and the upper ones one. It carries on the line of its shoot and nods over. A flower is a leathery orange calyx about 2.5 cm long, cut into five pointed teeth, and a trumpet up to 8 cm long: a tube narrow where it stands in the calyx and widening to a throat 3 cm across, and a limb 6.5 to 8 cm across of five broad rounded lobes, the two upper a little smaller. The tube is orange outside; the lobes are salmon-red; the throat is apricot with darker red lines. Four stamens, two long and two short, lie under the upper lobes with the style between them, and are drawn on the nearest model. The flowers open a few at a time from the base of the truss. A flower in bud is a club standing out of its calyx; it swells and spreads into the trumpet; the corolla then drops and leaves the calyx. The youngest flowers, at the tip, stay in bud to the end.

**Pod.** A stalked, spindle-shaped woody capsule 8 to 13 cm long and about 2 cm through, pointed, with a ridge along each of its two seams; green, drying to tan in late autumn, and hanging through the winter. One truss in twenty-five is given one, because pods are rare in Britain.

**Hold.** Tufts of short rootlets in two rows, below a node, on the side of a stem that lies against timber. They are drawn only where a framework stem holds a member by itself, and are hidden under the stem once it has thickened.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- All the year's free growth is drawn as flowering side shoots of one season. The plant also makes long non-flowering growths (Bean: "its long growths make it ungainly"), which are left out because the engine would keep each one for ever; and on an unpruned plant a shoot of the year stays as wood and branches the next year, which the engine cannot do, so the shoots are shed before each spring as a late-winter spur prune would remove them.
- A shoot of the year is drawn as a straight stiff line that rises a little. A real one arches out and over under the weight of its leaves and its truss.
- Every leaf in a run is drawn from one range of lengths, so the smaller leaves at the base and tip of a shoot are not smaller.
- The whole framework stops lengthening when the flowering shoots do, in late July. Real extension growth goes on into September.
- A truss opens from its base and its corollas drop in the same order; how many flowers are open at once follows from that and is more than on the plant, where two to five are usual. The bare axis of a spent truss, with its calyces, is drawn until the last corolla has gone and then not at all.
- A pod does not split. On the plant the two valves part in winter and the winged seeds blow out.
- Fruit is set in the same small share every year. On the plant it is none in most British years and some after a hot summer.
- The rootlets say nothing about whether the hold is strong enough. The profile's caveat stands: tie it in.

## Light, aspect and shelter

What the sources say, kept apart by what it is about. The light model shades and nothing else; warmth and shelter are reported here and are not modelled.

**Classes of light it accepts** (as the RHS defines them).

- *Full sun: yes, and it is what the plant is grown in.* RHS plant record: "Full sun". RHS growing guide: it "needs ... full sun in order to ripen the wood so it flowers freely" and "flowers best in full sun, in a warm sheltered spot". Trees and Shrubs Online, of 'Mme Galen': it "needs the sunniest wall and plenty of room".
- *Partial shade: it grows, and flowers less.* Burncoose Nurseries: "Full sun preferred. Tolerates partial shade in warmer areas. In colder areas, grow against a warm wall in full sun." Havlis nursery catalogue: partial shade gives "fewer and paler flowers". NC State Extension lists full sun or partial shade, for a climate with far hotter summers than Britain's. No British source offers partial shade for this plant.
- *Full shade: no source accepts it.*

**What the recommended aspect is for.** The RHS record gives "South-facing or West-facing" and "Sheltered". The reasons the sources give are warmth first and light second: full sun is asked for "to ripen the wood" (RHS guide), and the plant is "reasonably hardy if grown against a warm, sunny wall" (RHS record). In cooler temperate regions it "requires the shelter of a sunny wall to flower abundantly" (RHS A-Z Encyclopedia, as cited by Wikipedia). Bean says of the parent *C. grandiflora* that "in England it does not flower as spectacularly as on the Continent", and of *C. radicans* that pods form only "after unusually hot summers". So a south or west wall is asked for its heat, the sun it gets being part of how it gives that heat.

**Shelter.** From cold wind: the RHS guide says campsis "needs shelter from cold winds". From frost: late frosts can damage young growth (NC State), and in cold gardens there may be "foliage damage and stem dieback in harsh winters" (Burncoose). From wind and snow on the year's growth: "the wood is fragile, so long shoots are vulnerable to wind and snow" (Havlis).

**What shade does.** To growth: nothing read says that shade slows it; the sources that mention shade speak only of flowers. To flowering: fewer and paler flowers in partial shade (Havlis), and by the RHS's account unripened wood that does not flower freely.

**What too much sun or exposure does.** No source read reports scorch or any harm from sun. The harm reported is from cold and from drought at the root: "low temperatures or dry soil can cause flower buds to be shed" (RHS guide), although the established plant is "fairly drought tolerant" (RHS guide).

**For the model.** The light model reads the profile's sentence as accepting full sun only, which agrees with the British sources. That sentence ties flowering to summer heat, not to light, so the model does not reduce flowering in shade; the sources above would support doing so. Warmth is the stronger requirement and is not modelled at all: a run on a cool, exposed or north-facing structure will flower as freely as one on a south wall, and should not be believed on that point.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Strong substantial framework, prepared root-adhesion surface or explicit tie points; large open routes, not presumed self-adherent on a thin smooth metal wire. The RHS asks for a support at least 4 m high. The year's shoots stand 60 to 80 cm clear of the framework and need that room.

## Global structure placement logic

Sunny warm external face/edge, as a stronger late-summer flower alternative; poor match for cool exposed high canopy.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

After planting, cut all stems to 15 cm and train the strongest new shoots to fill the space (RHS). Once a framework has formed, spur-prune the side shoots in late winter to within two or three buds of the main stems (RHS); tie in young growths, carefully spaced (Trees and Shrubs Online); monitor vigorous shoots and contact integrity. Hard renovation to 30 cm reduces flowering (RHS).

## Interactions with the broader palette

Warm-season colour complement but can become a large competitor for wisteria and evergreens.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

H4 and heat preference reduce portability across UK climates; strong growth/woody mass, flower performance in cool summers uncertain. Suckers from the base and from running roots (Oates et al. 2014; White Flower Farm). Sap may irritate skin.

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

None of the growth numbers has been measured on a plant. The measurements that would settle the most:

- Shoots of the year per metre of framework on a spur-pruned plant, their length, and the share that end in a truss, in a British summer.
- Internode length on flowering shoots and on extension growth; leaf length along a shoot.
- Dates of budburst, first open flower, last open flower and leaf fall at a British site, over several years.
- Flowers to a truss, how many are open at once, days from first flower to last on one truss.
- Length and number of rootlets, how many nodes bear them, and what pull a rooted stem resists on the timber finish Bower uses: the contact/failed-contact test.
- Stem diameter at the base against age.
- How far a shoot of the year stands out before it arches, and how far it droops under a truss; the stiffness of the year's wood.
- Heat and insolation response of flowering; frequency of pods.

At minimum, bind all measured parameters to plant **age**, plant **part**, time step, site/climate, support properties and propagation/stock history. Keep missing values empty rather than extrapolating from species names or RHS maturity classes.
