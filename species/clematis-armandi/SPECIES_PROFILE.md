# Clematis armandi

**Common name:** Armand clematis (often informally spelt armandii)  
**Place in palette:** OPTION — sheltered evergreen early-spring fragrance

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Evergreen vigorous woody clematis. |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Twining leaf petioles. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 4–8 m; 5–10 years to ultimate size category. |
| Hardiness | H4 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Glossy trifoliate leaves, fragrant cream-white spring flowers; early to mid spring window (RHS), April according to Bean. |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun, south/west, **sheltered** from cold winds. |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained, neutral/alkaline commonly preferred. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates.

## Species-specific biological logic

Large evergreen foliage and spring flowering on established shoots; conserve appropriate old flower-bearing growth and avoid indiscriminate hard cutting.

## Form: what the model draws

What follows is drawn from published descriptions; every number is in [`morphology.json`](morphology.json) with its source, and nothing was measured on a plant.

**Habit.** A woody evergreen climber with stems to 6 m (Huang et al. 2024), 4 to 8 m high in gardens (RHS). It puts its strength into growing upwards and outwards: each year the older stems throw long shoots, which clasp what they touch and arch out and hang where they touch nothing. The foliage and the flowers are on the growth of the last two years, so the plant is a mass at the top and outside and goes bare below ("leave its lower reaches bare after a few seasons", International Clematis Society); left untrimmed, the old shoots build up into a thatch inside it.

**Stems.** Young shoots are ribbed (10 to 16 shallow grooves) and, as they emerge in spring, deep bronze-red (Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh); they turn green-brown as they ripen. Old stems are 1 to 5 cm thick, ridged along their length, the nodes swollen, with a brown stringy bark that comes away in strips (Kwantlen; the Hong Kong standard for the dried stem). The model draws a bronze-red shoot that browns in its first season and a ridged, straight-grained grey-brown bark from the second year on.

**Leaves.** In opposite pairs, each of three leaflets on a main stalk of 3.6 to 11 cm, the leaflets on stalks of their own, 1.3 to 2.5 cm long and twisted. A leaflet is 5 to 16 cm long and 1.5 to 7 cm wide, narrowly ovate to lance-shaped, the base rounded or a little heart-shaped, the tip drawn out to a point, the margin entire; leathery, hairless, dark glossy green, with three strong veins running from the base (Bean: "prominently three-veined"). New leaves are bronze. In photographs the leaflets hang nearly plumb from the end of the stalk, face out, a curtain of narrow dark blades, 0.22 to 0.42 as wide as long from plant to plant (morphology.json, with the files named). The model draws this: the main stalk stands out from the stem and bows over, the three leaflets hang from its end on bent stalks, the blade rounded into its base, long-sided and drawn in concave to a fine point, narrow on some leaves and broad on others; the three veins are fine pale lines lying in fine channels; the mature leaf is a dark, slightly grey green, bronze while it unfolds. A leaf lives through the season it is made in and the winter after and falls in June or July of the next year ("In summer the old leaves drop off the vine", Rainyside Gardeners), so last year's leaves stand behind the flowers in spring and the plant carries one year's leaves after midsummer.

**Shoots in the air.** The long shoots of the year that find nothing to hold are thin (4 mm at the tip, a taken figure near the 3.6 mm measured on the congener Clematis flammula), soft (the congener's 1.6 GN/m2 once set) and carry heavy leathery leaves, so they arch over within a reach of some 0.6 m and hang: the curtain of shoots photographs of an arch show. They do not branch and never die in the model.

**Flowers.** In March and April, from the leaf axils of the shoots made the summer before: "dense axillary clusters, mostly three on a stalk" (Bean), "usually paired along the stems and in bunches at the ends" (International Clematis Society). Each cluster rises from a cup of brown bud scales 0.8 to 2 cm long; its stalks are 0.4 to 8 cm long, each with narrow bracts where it forks, and the flower stalks 1.8 to 7 cm. A flower is 5 to 6 cm across (RHS), a flat star of four to seven narrow oblong sepals about 2.5 cm by 0.85 cm, pure or creamy white, round a boss of cream stamens 6 to 11 mm long and a tuft of silky styles, green-yellow in the middle; the buds are silvery. The flower stalks are red to maroon in photographs and in Curtis's Botanical Magazine t. 8587, and are drawn so. The scent is of almond or vanilla. One flower record of the model is one node: its two clusters, each of two or three stalks with one or three flowers, eleven to eighteen flowers in all, drawn 13 to 17 cm across. The flowers of a node open over the first third of its three weeks; sepals and stamens fall over the last half, leaving the stalks and the tuft of styles.

**Seed heads.** Where a flower sets, its styles lengthen into feathered tails 1.6 to 4.8 cm long on achenes 4 to 5 mm long: a silky tuft, green and silver at first, white and fluffy when ripe in June and July. The model sets heads on two flowering nodes in five (assumed), three to five heads to a node, swells them over five to seven weeks from petal fall, ripens them in June and early July and lets them blow away by mid September.

**Hold.** The stalks of the leaflets coil round a thin support that they touch and grip it (Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh). The model winds one at three leaf nodes in five, on stretches the plant holds by itself, which are cables: nothing stouter than 12 mm is taken to be clasped, so on laths and glulam the plant is tied.

**Through the year, at ten years old.** January: a dark evergreen mass on its support and a curtain of hanging leafy shoots, bare brown stems below and within. March and April: the same mass wreathed in white, the flowers standing out on red stalks in front of last year's leaves all along last year's shoots; bronze new shoots appear among them. May to July: long new shoots, bronze at first; silky seed heads where flowers set, whitening in June; last year's leaves fall in June and July. Late summer and autumn: the year's shoots in leaf, the older ones bare.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- One flower record is a whole node's display, and one fruit record a whole node's seed heads: the engine keeps one record to a node. The counts in a yearly account are therefore flowering nodes, each of a dozen flowers or more.
- An unopened flower is drawn as a small pale star and not as the closed, rounded, silvery bud it is: the drawing program can make a part grow and then drop it, but cannot show something in bud that is gone when the flower is open. The white does not flush rose as it ages (Bean: "changing to rose").
- The leaf has one size range; the smaller leaves of weak shoots are not drawn. Its underside is drawn like its upper side.
- A shoot of the year never branches and is never shed. The plant's real habit, new shoots from last year's shoots and old ones dying back inside, is replaced by new long shoots from the framework every year and old ones kept bare. The bare wood of a ten-year-old plant is therefore more than a gardener would leave, and is where a real plant would carry its youngest growth.
- A long shoot is never shed and never dies: once its leaves have fallen it hangs on as a bare brown string, and over ten years these build into more bare wood than a real plant, or a gardener, would leave. The gardener's routine under "Care" would take most of them out; it is not modelled.
- Stems thicken by the framework they carry and not by their long shoots, so old stems are drawn thinner (about 1.5 cm at ten years) than the 1 to 5 cm published.
- The wound stalk is drawn at the node, as a turn and a quarter round a 5 mm cable. Where several stems share a cable the engine lays them side by side, wider apart than the cable is thick, and a stalk on an outer stem is drawn beside the cable and not round it. Leaf stalks do not clasp neighbouring shoots, as they do on the plant.
- The flower stalks are drawn in the colour of the photographs; no flora gives it. No photograph of the seed heads of this species was found on Wikimedia Commons, so they are drawn from the flora's words alone.

## Light, aspect and shelter

- **Light classes accepted.** *Full sun*: every source (the RHS record gives full sun and nothing else; Clematis on the Web a sunny aspect; Gardeners' World full sun). *Partial shade*: sources disagree. North American extension services accept it (NC State: full sun to partial shade; Kwantlen and Oregon State: sun to part shade; Clemson: grows well in part shade in South Carolina), and the RHS guide to winter and spring clematis says of the group that it tolerates a more shaded spot; the International Clematis Society's list of clematis for north-facing sites puts "Clematis armandii and cultivars" under "NOT for partial shade". For Britain the RHS record's full sun only is the reading to keep. *Full shade*: no source accepts it.
- **What the recommended aspect is for.** The RHS asks for a south- or west-facing, sheltered position. That is asked chiefly for warmth and shelter, not for light: the plant is H4, "not particularly hardy" (Bean), its flower buds form in late summer and open in March, and the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh says it needs shelter "to avoid winter die back" and "to promote flower bud formation". Light matters too, for flowering and for scent (RHS guide: a sunny, sheltered spot for the best perfume).
- **What shade does.** To growth: little that is recorded; it climbs through shrubs and trees towards the light (the Edinburgh plant "gained five metres in height through a Viburnum tinus"), and its roots and base are best shaded (Rainyside Gardeners). To flowering: it flowers most freely in sun and shelter; in shade it is expected to grow but flower poorly (nursery and forum opinion, no measured figure), and clematis in partial shade begin to flower a little later (International Clematis Society).
- **What too much sun or exposure does.** No source reports sun scorch in Britain. The damage recorded is from cold and wind: cold, drying winter winds blacken the leaves, which then hang on the plant until summer (Rainyside Gardeners; Ashwood Nurseries: cold wind and severe frost scorch the evergreen foliage); a hard frost at flowering time destroys that year's flowers. In hot climates the roots want shade and leaf tips burn where soil or water is salty.
- **For the light model.** The profile's sentence ("Full sun, south or west facing, sheltered from cold winds.") reads as full sun only, south or west, shelter asked. Nothing here changes that reading. The aspect is mostly about warmth and wind, which the light model does not act on; the sources do tie flowering to sun, but the RHS record does not say so in words the reader of `taxon.json` would pick up.

Fine but adequately robust petiole-graspable frame or cable network; avoid unsupported exposure on tall free-standing roofs.

## Global structure placement logic

Mild-sheltered spring module near entrance/side facade, with plant support distinct from wisteria heavy stems.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Train into a bounded lane and prune after flowering as necessary; monitor cold damage and winter leaf condition. Left unpruned for several years it "can develop thatching that is hard to remove" (International Clematis Society); to clothe it lower down, one stem at least is cut back hard after flowering.

## Care: what the gardener does

RHS pruning group 1 (Rainyside Gardeners: "Pruning group 1 or A"; RHS, https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/clematis/group-one-pruning-guide). What the RHS guide says, and what follows for a Bower:

- **After planting.** Group 1 clematis are "pruned back hard the first spring after planting", to "a strong pair of leaf buds about 30cm (12in) above soil level" (RHS). Ashwood Nurseries give the same reason for this plant: unpruned it makes long single stems. The model starts from two stems on a 0.6 m cane and does not make this cut.
- **Every year, after flowering (mid to late May).** "For this group no regular pruning is required", but if needed "prune immediately after flowering in mid- to late spring": "Trim overlong branches and prune back dead or winter-damaged shoots to a pair of healthy buds" (RHS). Of this plant in particular, pruning is limited to "restricting them to the space available and removing, in spring, any leaves damaged by wind scorch"; it "does not usually respond well to renovation and pruning of established plants" (RHS).
- **Spring and summer.** "During the spring and summer, tie in new growth, spacing stems evenly on the support" (RHS). The model's trained plant is tied in as it grows, and nothing else.
- **What a gardener on a Bower would do, in the events the engine has.** From the second year, each year on 20 May, after the last flowers are over (they are over by the end of April in the model): `trim_to_bounds` with `freeBeyondM: 0.6`, which cuts off every long shoot that holds nothing and stands more than 0.6 m from where it left the structure. That is "restricting them to the space available" and takes out the old shoots that have flowered and are about to lose their leaves; the new shoots of the year are still short on that day and are kept, and they carry next spring's flowers. 0.6 m is this project's choice, the line a design allows. Tried in a scratch run on the specimen frame (six years): of 105 bare old long shoots on 15 January of the sixth year, 13 were left, while the leaves on that day (2576) and the flowering nodes of every year (802 in the sixth) were exactly those of the plant that was not cut, since the shoots that flower are the previous summer's. It takes the seed heads of the shoots it cuts. Not modelled today: only the routines in `MODELLED_ROUTINE` (packages/core/src/sim/planting.ts) are acted on, and this plant has none, so the trained portraits show a plant that is tied in and never cut.
- **Not modelled at all:** removing wind-scorched leaves in spring, and thinning the thatch of dead stems inside the plant (the engine never lets a shoot die).

## Interactions with the broader palette

Provides early spring evergreen + flowers where *Lonicera henryi* may offer foliage but not the same bloom; avoid overshading winter clematis.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

For taxon naming, **Kew accepts Clematis armandi**; earlier spelling armandii is widespread but not used as folder ID (confirmed 11 November 2026: IPNI record 709497-1, Kew's World Checklist and the RHS all use one final i). H4 winter performance depends on exposure. The RHS notes it does not usually respond well to renovation. Skin irritant; harmful to pets (rabbits) if eaten (RHS).

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

Petiole support grip, winter leaf retention, cold/wind damage, spring bud chronology, lateral extension and flowering density.

Measurements that would settle what the growth model still assumes: the length of a year's shoot and of its internodes at a known age; how many long shoots an established plant throws in a year and from where; how long a leaf lives; the share of last year's nodes that flower and the number of flowers to an axil; how many flowers ripen a seed head in Britain and how long the heads stay; the thickest support a leaflet stalk clasps; the thickness of stems of known age.
