# Trachelospermum jasminoides

**Common name:** Star jasmine  
**Place in palette:** CONDITIONAL — warm, sheltered evergreen fragrance

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Evergreen woody twiner. |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Stem twining (RHS: needs supporting wires or trellis); handedness is still not documented after a third search in November 2026. In the one photograph found of stems on canes (Wikimedia Commons, "Trachelospermum jasminoides A") the stems that can be followed cross the front of the cane from lower left to upper right, which would be a right-handed helix, but they are on a pot-grown plant wound on by hand and settle nothing. Aerial rootlets on damp rough surfaces are reported without citation and cannot be ruled out; if real, it could cling to timber as well as twine. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 8–12 m; 5–10 years to ultimate size category. The RHS growing guide is more guarded: "relatively slow growing", eventually "4m (14ft) or more". |
| Hardiness | H4 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Glossy dark leaves, often bronze/red in winter; exceptionally fragrant white flowers mid to late summer (RHS), in July and August (Bean). Fruit, a pair of slender follicles, is rare in Britain (RHS guide). |
| Aspect and exposure | Warm full sun/partial shade, ideally south/west, **sheltered from cold winds**. |
| Soil | Fertile, well-drained; dislikes soggy conditions and summer drought. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates.

## Form: what the model draws

Every shape number below is in [`morphology.json`](morphology.json) with its source and evidence status; every growth number is in [`model.json`](model.json). The drawing is `packages/viewer/src/plant/taxa/trachelospermum-jasminoides.ts`.

### The plant

**Stems.** Slender and twining. A shoot of the year is 2 to 3 mm thick, finely hairy and pale yellowish green, sometimes flushed bronze; in its second year it is smooth and red-brown with pale lenticels (Flora of China: "Stem brownish, lenticellate"), and it greys only slowly. The stems stay round and thin: the main stems of a ten-year-old plant are about as thick as a thumb at the base. They wind round a support and round one another, so that a wire, a cane or a lath comes to carry a rope of several. Cut, they bleed white latex.

**Leaves.** In opposite pairs, on stalks 3 to 12 mm long. The blade is elliptic, narrowly elliptic or ovate, 4.2 to 8.8 cm long and 1.6 to 3.4 cm wide on the one plant that has been measured, wedge-shaped at the base and drawn in to a short blunt point, with an entire margin a little rolled under. It is leathery, smooth and very glossy, dark green above with a pale midrib lying in a shallow groove and 6 to 12 pairs of faint side veins, paler beneath. The blade is 0.36 to 0.46 as wide as long and widest a little below the middle (read off photographs, which the measured middles agree with). It hangs from its short stalk: on a wall or frame the blades face out and a little up with their tips down, overlapping like shingles, whichever way the shoot runs; on a shoot out in the open they face the sky more. The model draws the blade flat out from its stalk and lets the viewer hold it so (the leaf's `hold`). New leaves are pale green, or bronzed in cool weather. In winter cold many leaves, most of them on the thin outer shoots, turn bronze to deep red; old leaves yellow and redden before they fall.

**Flowers.** In cymes: a slender stalk 2 to 6 cm long that forks by threes, each fork ending in a flower, two to nine flowers to a cyme. The cymes stand at the ends and in the upper leaf axils of short side shoots thrown by stems made in earlier years (Bean: "usually on short lateral twigs"), and are held out clear of the leaves, the flowers facing outwards and nodding a little. A flower is a narrow tube 5 to 10 mm long, swollen above the middle where the five stamens stand hidden, opening flat into five lobes as long as the tube, 1.7 to 2.3 cm across (2.5 cm in the RHS record). Each lobe is narrow where it leaves the throat and widest near its end, which is cut off at a slant and a little crisped; one edge is rolled under and the whole lobe is twisted like a propeller blade, so that the flower is a pinwheel turning anticlockwise as you face it. Pure white, creaming with age, with a small greenish-yellow eye. Five narrow green sepals, 2 to 5 mm, turned back at the tips. In bud the limb is furled into a pointed spindle with the edges of the lobes wound round it. Very fragrant.

**Fruit.** Two follicles from one flower on the cyme's stalk, hanging in a narrow V or standing stiffly apart at most of a right angle (both seen in photographs): linear, 10 to 25 cm long and 3 to 10 mm thick, tapering to a long point, smooth and pale green, drying dark brown to black and splitting down the inner side to release seeds 1.5 to 2 cm long, each with a tuft of silky hairs 1.5 to 4 cm long. Rarely set in Britain.

**How it holds on.** By its twining stems alone: no tendrils, no clasping leaf stalks, no prickles. Clinging rootlets are reported where a stem lies against a damp, rough surface (see below); they are not drawn.

### Through the year, as the model has it for Britain

| When | What is on the plant |
|---|---|
| December to March | In full leaf. Brown stems show between the leaves. On the rare plant that set fruit, the pods are brown from December and are gone by the end of March. No flowers. |
| Mid April to June | Growth starts in mid April: pale green shoots and leaves, full grown by mid June. From the start of June the young side shoots carry cymes in green bud. |
| End of June to early September | In flower. The first cymes open in the last days of June; each is in flower for two to four weeks, its flowers opening a few at a time; the display is at its fullest from mid July to mid August and the last petals fall in the first days of September. |
| July to September | Long twining shoots are thrown from the older stems and go on lengthening to the end of September. |
| September to November | In leaf, quiet. Where a cyme set fruit, the pair of green pods lengthens through the autumn. |

### Where the model simplifies

- **Winter colour is not shown.** The render data gives an evergreen leaf no change of colour, so the plant is the same green in January as in July. The bronze and red are in the look's palette for when the data can ask for them.
- **Leaf spacing.** Leaf pairs are 5 to 10 cm apart on the long shoots and the framework, 2 to 4 cm on the short flowering twigs, but the engine sets a node only at a stored point of a stem, 4 cm apart, so the twigs have a pair at every point. Leaves are taken to last three seasons; nobody has recorded how long they do. The oldest fall from mid May to the end of June, behind the new flush.
- **The nursery plant is one cane.** A plant bought on a 0.9 m cane is in truth three or more stems with side shoots and a hundred leaves or so; the engine starts it as one stem, so the first two seasons are thinner than a planted star jasmine is.
- **One cyme to a side shoot.** A real flowering shoot carries a cyme at its tip and one in each of its upper leaf axils; the engine allows one flower record at the end of a side shoot, and an inflorescence part-way along a twig cannot be placed. The model makes up the number with a twig at nearly every leaf node of the older stems.
- **Side shoots grow on but do not fork**, and leave their stem straight. On the plant they branch again each year, bend under their leaves and cymes and tangle into a mat a hand-span or two deep; the model lets them lengthen 5 to 15 cm a year to 0.4 m, which fills the depth but not the tangle.
- **A bud is a small flower.** Before it opens, a flower can only be drawn as its open form at three-tenths size, tinted green. The star jasmine's bud is a pointed, twisted spindle; the last two or three flowers of each cyme are drawn as such buds and do not open.
- **Flowers do not age** from white to cream, and a spent cyme vanishes whole on the day the engine calls it over; on the plant the corollas fall one by one and the stalk and calyces stay.
- **Pods do not split.** A ripe follicle turns brown and then is gone; the open pod with its plumed seeds is not drawn.
- **Long shoots that find nothing to wind round** are laid along the shape their weight gives them (`packages/core/src/sim/free-shoot.ts`): the measured searchers of this plant hold themselves out to about 1.1 m with a stiffness at their base of about 3000 MN/m2 (Vecchiato et al. 2023), and the model uses both; past that a shoot arches over and hangs, as the curtain off the cable stay shows in the stills. On the plant such a shoot also winds round its neighbours, and where it reaches the ground it roots ("The plant self-layers", Useful Temperate Plants); neither is modelled. A shoot never dies back, so in a run without the gardener's spring visit (below) they stay, bare once their leaves have gone.
- **Rootlets.** Left out. The engine sets clinging rootlets only on stretches a plant holds by itself, which for this twiner means cables, the one place it would not root.
- **Twining direction** is unknown; the engine winds it right-handed in order to draw it at all.
- **Young shoots in a mixed planting** are drawn at one thickness for every plant in the run, whichever plant's it happens to be.

## Light, aspect and shelter

What the sources say, clause by clause. The light model reads only the sentence in `taxon.json` (`site.aspect`), which is unchanged; this section is the evidence behind it and what it leaves out.

| Question | What is recorded | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Classes of light it accepts | **Full sun and partial shade.** Full shade is not listed. | RHS plant record: "Full sun, Partial shade". RHS growing guide: "Sun or light shade is best". Useful Temperate Plants (citing Huxley): "in full sun or with part-day shade". BBC Gardeners' World: best in full sun, copes with partial shade. |
| Aspect | South, west or east facing; south or west "ideal". North is not listed. | RHS plant record: "West–facing or South–facing or East–facing". RHS growing guide: "A south- or west-facing wall or fence is ideal". |
| What the aspect is asked for | **Warmth and shelter, not light.** The same sources that ask for a south or west wall accept partial shade; what they rule out is cold: "Avoid frost-prone or cold, exposed spots"; "with shelter from cold, drying winds"; hardiness H4. An east wall is accepted by the RHS record although it is the coldest of the three in winter, and a plant in Belgrade kept on a north-east facing screen between tall buildings, out of the two prevailing cold winds, came through -15.5 degC (Ocokoljic et al. 2026). | RHS plant record and growing guide; Ocokoljic et al. 2026. |
| Exposure | **Sheltered.** | RHS plant record: "Sheltered"; "with shelter from cold winds". |
| What shade does to growth | Not stated outright by any source read. In the wild it grows on "Sunny edges of forests, brushwoods" (Flora of China): a plant of the lit edge, not of the forest floor. | Flora of China. |
| What shade does to flowering | Fewer flowers. "For best flowers, grow in fertile, well-drained soil in a sheltered, sunny spot" (NC State Extension). One uncited statement has it flowering in full sun, partial shade or total shade (Wikipedia), and Useful Temperate Plants shows a photograph of it flowering well in a shady place; nobody gives a number. So: flowering falls with shade and does not stop in partial shade. | NC State Extension; Useful Temperate Plants. |
| Too much sun | Outdoors in Britain, none recorded: full sun is the first choice. Under glass, "keep it out of direct sun in summer, as this may scorch the leaves" (RHS guide). In hot spots outdoors it draws scale insects and red spider mite (RHS guide). | RHS growing guide. |
| Too much exposure | Cold, drying wind is the limit. In cold snaps "the leaves may turn red, develop spots or even fall" (RHS guide), and the plant recovers except after the severest winters. Young plants are the more tender: "It is rather slow-growing when young, and then more vulnerable to frost than when it is established" (Bean). The winter reddening the RHS record gives as an ornament ("often turning deep bronze-red in winter") is the mild end of the same response. | RHS growing guide; Bean. |

**For the light model:** accept full sun and partial shade; hold growth back below partial shade; tie flowering to sun (a clause the present sentence in `taxon.json` does not carry, so the model does not yet apply it to this plant). **Not light, and not modelled:** the south or west aspect and the shelter are about winter cold and wind. A bright but wind-swept crown fails this plant's requirement while passing the light test.

## Species-specific biological logic

Evergreen leaf cohorts and woody extension with sensitivity to cold-wind exposure; separate survival from seasonal ornamental leaf appearance.

The flowers are carried on short side shoots that break from stems made in earlier years, so the plant flowers where it has older wood and not at the tips of its newest growth; the engine's flowering side shoots stand for them. A few cymes also form in the leaf axils of the year's long shoots (Flora of China: "terminal and axillary").

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Regular fine to medium cables or timber laths; sturdy tie-in during early establishment; no assumed ability to cling to flat timber.

## Global structure placement logic

Sheltered sunny edge/entrance, courtyard or lower microclimate. Avoid asserting it will thrive on an exposed high freestanding crown.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Train/tie onto the lattice, manage spread, inspect post-winter leaf scorch and potential dieback; replace only where unsuited.

RHS pruning group 11, in early spring: maintenance pruning is rarely needed and consists of thinning congested, weak or damaged shoots. Mulch once a year, in spring or autumn. Flowering wood is provisionally the current season's growth, on short side shoots from older stems: Bean has the cymes "usually on short lateral twigs", and Useful Temperate Plants, citing Huxley's RHS Dictionary of Gardening, says "Flowers are produced on short laterals that grow from old wood". The RHS spring timing agrees; NC State Extension's advice to prune after flowering points the other way. Either way a hard cut into the older stems costs the flowers that those stems would have thrown.

The long shoots of summer that find no support are the gardener's to tie in or to cut off in spring; the model has no routine for this plant, so in a run they stay.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Useful summer-scent counterpoint to spring wisteria; possible overlap with evergreen honeysuckle, so avoid unnecessary competition.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

H4 hardiness describes cold survival, not exposed-wind tolerance. Exposed sites may turn sparse or suffer damage. The whole plant is poisonous (Flora of China); white latex when cut.

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

Wind/cold leaf retention, flowering threshold and flower density, support grasping preference, trained growth, water stress.

What a measurement would settle, in the order the model leans on it:

1. **The largest support a shoot will wind round unaided** (mm): whether it takes 40 mm laths by itself decides how much tying a Bower needs. A row of canes and laths of graded thickness beside one plant for a season.
2. **Twining direction**: one look at an untrained shoot on a cane.
3. **Annual extension of a main stem and of a side branch** in a British garden, by year from planting (m): the leader rate, the branch rates and the three establishment factors are all choices.
4. **Leaf pairs to the metre** on long shoots and on short ones, and **short flowering shoots to the metre of older stem**: the two numbers that decide how dense the plant is.
5. **How long a leaf lives** (tag leaves, count yearly), and **what share turn red in which winter**.
6. **Cymes to a flowering shoot and the share of shoots that flower**, in sun and in shade.
7. **How long one cyme is in flower**, and first and last flowering dates in Britain over several years.
8. **Whether rootlets form on timber**, and on what finish.
9. **Diameter of stems by age**, for the weight they put on a lath.

At minimum, bind all measured parameters to plant **age**, plant **part**, time step, site/climate, support properties and propagation/stock history. Keep missing values empty rather than extrapolating from species names or RHS maturity classes.
