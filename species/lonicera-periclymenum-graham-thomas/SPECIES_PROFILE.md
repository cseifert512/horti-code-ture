# Lonicera periclymenum 'Graham Thomas'

**Common name:** Common honeysuckle or woodbine, cultivar 'Graham Thomas'  
**Place in palette:** CORE OPTION — biodiversity and fragrant summer flowering

A selection of the native woodbine found in a Warwickshire hedge about 1960 and named for Graham Stuart Thomas (Bean's supplement; Ashridge). It differs from the wild plant in its vigour, in its large flowers, and in having no red in them: they open white and turn buff yellow.

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Large, deciduous, twining woody climber (RHS); "exceptionally vigorous" (Bean's supplement). In sheltered places some leaves of the species stay green through winter (BSBI account). |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Stem twining, left-handed helix: clockwise seen from above (Wildlife Trusts, First Nature; no peer-reviewed species-level source retrieved). The stems wrap round their support and round each other (BSBI account). Darwin saw the species "twining up a young beech-tree nearly 4 1/2 inches in diameter", the only English twiner he had seen twine round a tree: 114 mm is the thickest support it is recorded as holding, not a measured limit. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 4–8 m tall, 1–1.5 m spread; 5–10 years to ultimate size. |
| Hardiness | H6 (RHS). |
| Leaf | Opposite, simple, entire; ovate, oval or obovate, 38–64 mm long and 25–38 mm wide, mostly pointed; lower pairs stalked, uppermost stalkless and never joined round the stem (Bean, of the species). Dark green above, whitish beneath (RHS, of the cultivar). |
| Leaf season | New leaves begin to open in January (December in the BSBI account) and are well grown in April; they fall in November (Beckett and Beckett 1979, of the species). No autumn colour: the leaves drop green (UConn). |
| Flower | Terminal stalked heads of close whorls, 6–20 flowers to a head; each flower 40–50 mm long, a slender tube and two rolled-back lips, the upper of four lobes, with five long stamens and a longer style (Bean; BSBI account; Flora of New Zealand). White, fading to buff yellow, with no red (RHS; Bean's supplement). Fragrant, most strongly in the evening. |

| Fruit | Red berries (RHS), 8 mm across, globose, stalkless in tight clusters, ripe from mid to late August, some hanging until October or early November (BSBI account, of the species). |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun or partial shade; any aspect; exposed or sheltered (RHS). See "Light, aspect and shelter". |

## Light, aspect and shelter

- **Light classes accepted:** full sun and partial shade (RHS record of the cultivar). The RHS defines full sun as more than six hours of direct sun a day at midsummer, and partial shade as three to six. It does not list full shade. Its cultivation note says the plant grows best in partial shade but tolerates full sun.
- **Aspect:** north-, west-, south- or east-facing (RHS record): every aspect is accepted, so no aspect is asked for, whether for light, for warmth or for shelter. The plant is hardy to H6.
- **Exposure:** exposed or sheltered (RHS record); the RHS also suggests it for coastal sites.
- **What shade does to growth:** the species persists in deep shade, as a sparse, few-leaved plant whose stems creep and root along the ground instead of climbing (BSBI account, after Grime). Plants succeed even in quite deep shade (Grey-Wilson and Matthews 1983, as given by the Useful Temperate Plants database).
- **What shade does to flowering:** the RHS honeysuckle guide has "the best show of flowers" in dappled shade and warns that "too much shade will result in an absence of flowers". Of the wild plant: "plants in deeper shade do not flower at all", while plants in full open sunlight flower from May to late September (BSBI account). Flowering therefore answers to light, though the profile's one-line aspect sentence does not say so.
- **What too much sun does:** on a south-facing wall, in sun all day, "the leaves may be scorched and the plant more prone to powdery mildew"; honeysuckles are "more prone to powdery mildew during hot dry weather", and the foot of a wall is dry (RHS honeysuckle guide). The remedy given is moisture at the root: a mulch, and the old rule of roots in shade and shoots in the sun (Huxley 1992, in the Useful Temperate Plants database). The condition is heat and dryness at the root, not light as such.

The light model shades and nothing else (CLAUDE.md, "Light is not warmth"): scorch, mildew and drought at a wall foot are reported here and are not modelled.

## Form: what the model draws

**Through the year.** In December the plant is a tangle of bare, slender, tan twigs on grey-brown, stringy older stems. From mid January the leaves break, bluish green, and by mid April the side shoots of earlier years are in full leaf, weeks before most climbers; new side shoots lengthen from the old stems through spring. From early June each side shoot that flowers ends in a stalked head held out on the line of the shoot, above the small round pair of leaves that stands free beneath it. The head lengthens out of a knot of erect, club-shaped, cream buds; its lowest whorl opens first, white, and has turned buff yellow by the time the next whorl opens, so a head carries both colours at once, with long stamens and longer styles standing out all round. Heads follow one another until early October, later ones on the growth of the year. Where a head has gone over, a tight cluster of glossy berries swells, green and then red, from August into November. The leaves dull and yellow a little in late October and are off by the end of November.

**The parts.** Leaves stand in opposite pairs: stalked and pointed on the long shoots, stalkless and blunter on the flowering side shoots (Bean's two kinds of leaf). Stems twine in a left-handed coil where they hold a support themselves and are laid along it where a gardener ties them in. There are no tendrils, rootlets or prickles.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- One leaf size and one node spacing for the whole plant: the long shoots of the real plant have longer internodes and larger leaves than its side shoots.
- Every pair of leaves lies in the same plane as the pair before it; on the real shoot each pair stands at right angles to the last.
- A leaf has one colour on both faces, so the whitish underside is not drawn.
- A side shoot is made in one season and then only re-leafs and re-flowers; on the real plant it branches and becomes part of the framework. Long shoots of the year (the RHS's "wayward long shoots") are neither shed nor clothed in later years, so the model makes few of them.
- The head is one model that opens whorl by whorl. Its flowers cannot change colour one by one, so the whorl that opened first is painted yellow from the start; the buds of the middle stay buds to the end; a spent flower drops clean instead of hanging on orange-brown.
- A head on the growth of the year sits at a leaf node; on the real plant it ends a shoot. Heads at the ends of side shoots open evenly through the season, where the real plant has a main flush and then a succession.
- Berries ripen within a window of dates, not a number of weeks after their own flowers.
- The semi-evergreen habit of sheltered plants is not shown: every leaf is off by the end of November.
- Shoots that find no support are drawn by the engine as it draws every plant's: see the run's own notes.

## Species-specific biological logic

Flowers at the ends of short side shoots from last year's wood (RHS honeysuckle guide), and again on the growth of the year (Bean's supplement: it still flowers from June after a winter cut). Stem twining and a winter-deciduous state, with a very early leaf. Berries follow the flowers and are kept as a wildlife and public-safety output.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Something to twine no thicker than a stout pole: laths and cables it holds by itself; glulam it has to be tied to. Access for cutting vigorous long shoots and for seeing where the berries are.

## Global structure placement logic

Perimeter/lower-to-middle lane with flowers close enough to visitors to smell, plus ecological benefit for pollinators. Not in deep shade, where it will not flower; not at the dry foot of a wall in sun all day.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Prune/control after flowering as needed and maintain distinct lane from evergreen honeysuckle.

RHS pruning group 11, after flowering: cut flowered shoots back by a third in late summer (RHS honeysuckle guide). A plant that has become congested and bare at the bottom is renovated by cutting every stem to about 60 cm in late winter and choosing among the shoots that follow. Flowering wood is recorded as both, provisionally: the RHS and Gardens Illustrated say the flowers come on side shoots from the previous year's stems, while Bean says this cultivar flowers from June to October even when cut back in winter, which means later flowers on new growth.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Adds later fragrant season to wisteria; do not conflate native species ecology with non-native commercial cultivars in every jurisdiction.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

Berry is ornamental and not to be eaten (RHS); of low toxicity (BSBI account, after Cooper and Johnson). Can entangle neighbours, and its coils can distort the young stems of woody plants it climbs (BSBI account). Aphids on young shoots in spring; powdery mildew in hot dry weather (RHS).
