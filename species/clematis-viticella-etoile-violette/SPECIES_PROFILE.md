# Clematis viticella 'Étoile Violette'

**Common name:** Purple summer clematis 'Étoile Violette'  
**Accepted name:** RHS: *Clematis* 'Étoile Violette' (Vt) (record 92936); *Clematis viticella* 'Étoile Violette' is an RHS synonym. Raised by Francisque Morel, France, introduced 1885 (Clematis on the Web). Bean calls it "obviously a hybrid" of *C. viticella*.  
**Place in palette:** CORE — summer floral module

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Large, vigorous deciduous clematis (RHS); Group 3 pruning. Of *C. viticella*, Bean: "partly woody", the summer growth dying back in winter nearly to the older stem. |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Leaf stalks and the stalks of the leaflets clasp slender supports; the stems do not twine (Darwin 1875; Bean). |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 2.5–4 m high, 1–1.5 m spread; generally 2–5 years to ultimate size class. Clematis on the Web 2.5–4 m; International Clematis Society 10–15 ft. |
| Hardiness | H6 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Single flowers 5–10 cm across of four to six broad, velvety, deep purple tepals round a small boss of cream stamens (RHS; Clematis on the Web), semi-nodding (HelpMeFind), "mostly on the upper half of the new wood" (International Clematis Society); mid to late summer (RHS), July to September (Clematis on the Web). Mid-green leaves of five leaflets (RHS). Seed heads of the species: broad, short achenes with very small tails that are not feathery (Bean). Winter stems leafless. |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun or partial shade; any aspect; exposed or sheltered (RHS). See "Light, aspect and shelter". |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained; neutral/alkaline preferred. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates. Several characters are recorded for *C. viticella* and not for the cultivar; `morphology.json` says which.

## Species-specific biological logic

Flowers on **current-season** stems. Hard pruning in late winter affects current growth architecture but does not remove the prior-year flowering cohort as in Group 1 clematis. The whole plant above the stubs is made new each year: what it looks like in July is what it grew since March.

## Light, aspect and shelter

- **Light classes accepted:** full sun and partial shade (RHS plant record). The RHS defines full sun as more than six hours of direct sun a day at midsummer and partial shade as three to six; anything under two hours it calls deep shade. Full shade is not among the classes the RHS lists for this plant.
- **Aspect:** any: south-, north-, west- or east-facing (RHS; Clematis on the Web "any aspect"). BBC Gardeners' World gives south or west facing, which reads as the sunnier choice for flower, not as a need for warmth: the plant is H6 and the RHS lists it for exposed as well as sheltered sites. Nothing read asks for an aspect for warmth or for shelter.
- **What shade does:** the RHS says of clematis in general that flowering is "normally disappointing in a very shady spot". No source read says what shade does to this cultivar's growth, or by how much flowering falls in partial shade: both unknown.
- **What too much sun does:** the RHS wants the roots and the base of the plant kept cool and shaded (by planting, or a layer of pebbles) and the soil moist; Bean notes that clematis flower in the sun with their lower parts in shade. The RHS says the flowers of *some early-summer* large-flowered cultivars fade early in too bright a spot; this is not said of 'Étoile Violette' or of the Viticella Group. A facade-greening supplier's page (not loaded; seen only in a search summary) says viticellas dislike hot walls: unverified.
- **For the light model:** flowering needs sun on the top growth; the foot wants shade. The second is a matter of soil temperature and moisture, not of light for the leaves, and should not be folded into a light requirement.

## Form: what the model draws

**Through the year.** In winter the plant is a sheaf of slender brown stems of last year, bare, tied along the timber or hanging by their hardened leaf stalks from a cable. On 20 February the gardener cuts every one to 0.25 m. From mid March each stub throws four to seven shoots, which make 2.5 to 3.3 m by late July: ribbed, green flushed red-brown, with a pair of leaves every 8 to 13 cm. A leaf is 10 to 13 cm long, pinnate on a wiry axis that kinks downward at each pair of leaflets: five stalked leaflets as a rule (RHS), some of them lobed, some leaves with a third pair or with the lowest pair divided in three (Bean, Darwin), a few of three leaflets only. Where a stem holds a cable by itself, six nodes in ten have a leaf stalk wound round it. From early May the nodes laid down beyond the first 1.4 m of each stem form flower buds, each opening about seven weeks after it is laid down, so that the lower part of every stem stays in leaf only. A flowering node carries a cyme: a stalk from the leaf axil, a pair of small leafy bracts, and three long-stalked flowers, the end one opening first and the side flowers a week or so after. Buds are ovoid, pointed and nodding; a flower opens through a bell to nearly flat, 6.5 to 9.5 cm across, facing outward and a little down; four to six broad tepals, long-pointed, tip and margins turned back, deep violet with a redder band down the middle, round a small cream boss. The tepals drop one by one, then the stamens, leaving a tuft of styles. The first flower opens at the end of June, the display is at its height in the first three weeks of August and is over by mid September. Half the cymes keep seed: small round heads of flat green achenes with short bare tails, browning through September and October and dropping by Christmas. The leaves dull and yellow from mid October and fall through November.

Left unpruned, the model keeps its stems: they stop at their length, are bare below a metre, and each spring about two nodes in three above that break into leafy shoots 0.3 to 1 m long that end in a cyme and die back in winter (Bean). Flowering is thinner and higher, as the RHS describes. Untied, the plant has only what its leaf stalks can clasp: on the specimen frame, the one cable.

**Shoots that stand free.** A plant cut down each year and tied in has none: every stem lies along timber or cable. An unpruned plant's new shoots stand free of the support. In life they are lax: a clematis stem is slender (Bean), a few millimetres thick, and cannot hold itself out; a shoot leans a hand's length from the stem, then arches and hangs under its leaves and flowers, or clasps whatever it meets, and stems that outgrow their support "bend and trail" (International Clematis Society). The engine draws them straight and stiff, which is its fault and not the plant's; their number and length are not reduced to hide it. No source gives the stiffness or density of the stems.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- *Branching.* A real stem throws leafy flowering side branches from its upper nodes in the season it is made. The engine does not branch a shoot in its first season, so those branches are stood in for by more stems from the base (four to seven a stub) and by drawing each flowering node as a cyme of three. Total flower count is of the right order; its arrangement is tidier than life, a row of cymes along each stem instead of a foam of branchlets.
- *One flower record to a node.* A node has two axils, each with one to three flowers. The model draws three at a node.
- *Flowers face one way.* The engine faces every cyme away from its support and 20 degrees up. Real flowers turn to the light.
- *Opening.* A bud is exact from a week before it opens; younger buds are drawn a little open at the tip. A falling tepal is drawn folding shut and thinning to nothing, not dropping.
- *Seed heads* appear at nothing and swell on new stalks when the cyme is over, because a flower record ends before a fruit record begins.
- *Leaves* are one size on the whole plant; the smaller, simpler leaves near the flowers are a variant chosen at random, not by position.
- *Autumn.* No autumn colour is claimed. Dead leaves do not hang on the stems as they often do in life.
- *The clasp* is drawn round a support standing upright, because the render data does not say which way the cable runs; on a level cable it is wound the wrong way.
- *Stems from one stub* are laid side by side at 22 mm centres however many there are, so on a cable they run as a flat band beside it.
- *When flowers open.* A node's cyme opens on a day drawn evenly between seven weeks after the node was laid down and 16 August, so the display builds to a peak in mid August and stops; in life it peaks earlier and tails off.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Fine wires / thin lattice capable of being grasped by developing petioles; prune access right from its basal crown. In the model the plant holds a 5 mm cable by itself and has to be tied to any timber.

## Global structure placement logic

Lower 2.5–4 m, ideally visually prominent under the woody canopy and on separate narrow supports.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Group 3 cut back in late winter/early spring (RHS: to the lowest pair of healthy buds, 15–30 cm above the soil, in February or March), then retrain and monitor for canopy entanglement; can be repeated annually. RHS: guide the stems so that they spread evenly.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Complements wisteria after its spring display, provided adequate summer light reaches the clematis.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

Do not assign wisteria twining/flower-spur rules; root competition and shading may strongly reduce flowers. Skin irritant; harmful to pets (rabbits) if eaten (RHS).

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

None of these was found in any source and each would be settled by a season's measurement on one established plant:

- stems from the base each year, and how many side branches each makes (count in July);
- length of stem between leaf pairs, low and high on a stem; thickness of a stem at its base and tip;
- length of the leaf stalk, the leaflet stalks and the flower stalks; width of leaflets; tepal length and width;
- flowers per node and per plant; how long one flower lasts; dates of first and last flower;
- the share of flowers that set seed, the width of a seed head, and when the seed drops;
- the stoutest support a leaf stalk clasps;
- what partial shade does to the number of flowers.

Bind all measured parameters to plant **age**, plant **part**, time step, site/climate, support properties and propagation/stock history. Keep missing values empty rather than extrapolating from species names or RHS maturity classes.
