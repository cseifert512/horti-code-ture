# Clematis 'Princess Diana'

**Common name:** Pink texensis-type clematis 'Princess Diana'  
**Accepted name:** RHS: *Clematis* 'Princess Diana' (T), Texensis Group (record 118677).  
**Origin:** 'Bees' Jubilee' x *C. texensis*, raised by Barry Fretwell (Peveril Nursery, Devon), introduced 1984 as 'The Princess of Wales' and renamed in 1996 (Clematis on the Web). RHS Award of Garden Merit.  
**Place in palette:** OPTION — vivid pink summer accent

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Deciduous climber of herbaceous habit (RHS); only the base is woody and the top growth mostly dies back in winter (Bean, of *C. texensis* in Britain). Group 3 pruning. |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Twining leaf stalks (RHS). |
| Height | 2.0–2.5 m from a plant cut down each year (Clematis on the Web; Graham Rice for the RHS); RHS ultimate-height band 2.5–4 m, 2–5 years to reach it; spread 0.5–1 m. |
| Hardiness | H5 (RHS). |
| Leaf | Usually pinnate (RHS, of the Texensis Group); light green (International Clematis Society). |
| Flower | Single, tulip-shaped, to 6 cm long, luminous deep pink with a deeper bar, paler at the margin outside, cream and maroon stamens (RHS; International Clematis Register). Mid summer to early autumn (RHS); July to October (Clematis on the Web; Graham Rice for the RHS). |
| Fruit | Silky seed heads, "gorgeous and persistent" (International Clematis Society). |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun or partial shade; south, north, west or east facing; exposed or sheltered (RHS). See "Light, aspect and shelter". |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained; chalk, clay, loam or sand; alkaline or neutral (RHS). |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. Nothing in this file has been measured on a plant.

## Form: what the model draws

Through the year. In February the gardener cuts every stem to a stub 15–30 cm long (RHS group 3), and what is left is a small woody base, grey-brown and stringy. From mid March the stubs and the crown throw new shoots: thin, ridged, wiry, green and flushed maroon on the sunny side, with a pair of leaves at every joint, 8 to 16 cm apart. A leaf is 11 to 17 cm long: a stalk, then three leaflets, or five on the longer leaves, each on a stalk of its own; a leaflet is broadly ovate with a rounded to heart-shaped base and a short point, entire, 4 to 7.5 cm long and about three quarters as wide, light to mid green, with three main veins from its base, and now and then lobed on one side like a mitten. The plant holds on by its leaf stalks, which take a turn round anything thin enough: here the 5 mm cable, and each other. By July the stems are 2 to 2.6 m long and the flowers begin: each stands up and out on a stalk about two flower-lengths long that carries a pair of small leafy bracts. The bud is egg-shaped, pointed, ribbed and downy, green and then pink. The flower is a tulip of four thick pointed tepals 5 to 6 cm long, held together as a swollen tube for a little under half their length and turned back above; as it ages it flares to a trumpet 5 to 7 cm across. Inside it is a luminous deep pink, slightly mauve, with a deeper bar down each tepal; outside, pinkish red along three ribs with a pale margin. A boss of thirty to forty cream stamens, the outermost flushed maroon, stands in the tube. Flowers come in succession into October. When the tepals drop the stamens and styles are left as a brush, and where the flower set, the styles lengthen into the tails of a seed head: a ball of twenty to thirty achenes 5 to 7 cm across, green and silky at first, drying to silver-gold fluff that stays on the dead stalks into winter. In October and November the leaves yellow, brown and fall, the stems ripen to a pale stringy brown, and the whole top stands dead until it is cut down again.

Where the model simplifies.

- **Branching.** On the plant a stem branches in the season it is made: short flowering shoots leave the upper leaf joints, two to a joint, each ending in a flower with one or two more from the axils of its bracts (Gibson's account of the parent species). The engine cannot branch a stem in its first season. The model stands in for this with more stems from the base (a crown of seven stubs, each giving two or three shoots) and with one long-stalked flower at nearly every joint, the stalk and its pair of bracts being drawn as part of the flower. So the lower third of a stem carries flowers in the model where on the plant it carries none, and a joint carries one flower where it may carry two to six.
- **Opening wider with age.** The flower model cannot change its shape while it is open, so each flower is drawn in one of four states, from just open to flared wide, and a plant shows all four at once.
- **The bud.** An unopened flower is drawn as the open one at three tenths of its size (the organ program's rule). The real bud is two thirds as long as the open flower.
- **One leaf size.** The engine gives a taxon one range of leaf length, so the small leaves and bracts near the flowers are full-size leaves in the model; only the pair of bracts on each flower stalk is small.
- **Stems lie where the structure is.** Stems follow members and cables exactly, in straight parallel lines 22 mm apart where several share one; on the plant they wander, cross and bundle together.
- **Leaf stalks.** The stalk of a leaf is drawn green; on the plant it is often maroon. A clasping stalk is drawn as a separate coil beside its leaf, and is drawn right only round an upright wire: the data does not say which way the thing it holds runs.
- **Untended.** Left unpruned, the plant in the model keeps every old stem alive and makes its new growth as shoots from the upper joints of those stems, hanging free in the air. On the plant most of the top dies back in winter and new shoots come from the base as well, climbing through the dead growth and clasping it.

## Light, aspect and shelter

- **Light classes accepted.** The RHS record gives full sun or partial shade (as the RHS defines them: more than six hours of direct sun a day at midsummer, and three to six). It does not list full shade.
- **Specialist growers ask for more.** Rogerson Clematis Garden: *C. texensis* and all its hybrids, this cultivar named, need full sun from the ground up to flower well, and like a hot site with a gravel mulch. The International Clematis Society's recommended list: the Texensis Group needs full sun in a sunny, airy position. Bean, of the group's older hybrids: a sunny position. The Chicago Botanic Garden trial that rated it was in full sun.
- **Aspect.** The RHS record lists south, north, west and east facing, all four. No source asks for a particular aspect for this cultivar; where specialists ask for sun and a "hot site" it is for light and for warmth together. Warmth is not light and is not modelled.
- **Shelter.** The RHS record says exposed or sheltered: no shelter is asked for. An airy position is asked for against mildew.
- **What shade does.** To growth: nothing is recorded. To flowering: the RHS growing guide says that flowering is normally disappointing in a very shady spot, and the specialists above tie good flowering to full sun. Shade and still air also favour powdery mildew, to which the Texensis Group is prone (this cultivar less than others: International Clematis Society).
- **What too much sun or heat does.** Roots and the base of the plant are to be kept cool and shaded (RHS). In the southern United States, leaves turn brown and crinkled where days pass 33 °C without cooler nights (International Clematis Society); not a British condition. Water-stressed plants are more prone to mildew (RHS).
- **What the model does with this.** The light model reads the sentence in `taxon.json` ("Full sun or partial shade; broadly adaptable"), which is the RHS's and is left as it is. It therefore lets the plant grow unchecked down to the shady end of partial shade and does not tie its flowering to sun. The specialists' "full sun to flower well" is recorded here and is not yet in that sentence.

Left untended on the same frame it holds only the 5 mm cable: two stems climb it in the first year and stay; from then on about seven new shoots a year, 0.4 to 1.2 m long, break from those stems above 1.2 m and carry 50 to 90 leaves and 15 to 40 flowers. This agrees in kind with the RHS's account of an unpruned group 3 clematis ("top heavy, flowering well above eye level, and with a bare base") and is thinner than a real neglected plant, for the reasons given under "Untended" above.

## Species-specific biological logic

Flowers on current-year stems (Group 3) and regenerates from the base after late-winter pruning. A flower exists only where a stem of the year formed a bud; a seed head only where a flower went over on the plant.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Something thin to clasp: cable, wire or rods no thicker than about 12 mm (published advice ranges from 6 to 19 mm; not measured for this plant). It cannot hold a 40 mm lath or a glulam member and has to be tied to them. Separate lane from big woody wisteria arms; low enough for annual pruning and retraining.

## Global structure placement logic

Visitor-level colour as an alternative or companion to *Étoile Violette*; use discrete colour fields rather than chaotic co-mingling. Its flowers stand up and out on long stalks, so they show best from the side and from a little above, at or below eye level.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Hard prune in February or March to the lowest pair of healthy buds, 15–30 cm above the soil (RHS group 3), then tie in the new growth, spacing the stems evenly.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Affects summer colour rather than structural woody architecture; competition with other clematis and honeysuckle requires management.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

Do not model as a self-twining wisteria. Skin irritant; harmful to pets (rabbits) if eaten (RHS). Powdery mildew in still, shaded or dry positions.

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

Stems from the crown at each age; stem length and joint spacing through a season; leaves and leaflets measured (length, width, stalks, how many of three and of five); flowers to a stem and where on it; how long a flower lasts; share of flowers that set a seed head; the thickest support a leaf stalk clasps; what an unpruned plant keeps alive over a British winter. Bind every measurement to plant **age**, plant **part**, date, site and stock.
