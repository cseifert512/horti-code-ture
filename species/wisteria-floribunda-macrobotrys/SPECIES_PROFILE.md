# Wisteria floribunda 'Macrobotrys'

**Common name:** Long-racemed Japanese wisteria  
**Accepted name:** RHS: *Wisteria floribunda* f. *multijuga* 'Kyushaku' (record 372711). 'Macrobotrys' and 'Multijuga' are RHS synonyms of it; the working label is kept because it is the name the plant is sold under.  
**Place in palette:** PRIMARY — alternate architectural wisteria species/cultivar

## Observational / horticultural baseline

| Trait | Currently supported description |
|---|---|
| Growth habit | Deciduous, woody, twining climber |
| Climbing or support mechanism | Stem twining, **left-handed** helix: clockwise seen from above. The RHS and Bean say clockwise without stating a viewpoint; Valder (1995) and Japanese floras supply it (clockwise from above, an S-twist). The opposite of *W. sinensis*. One source read (US Forest Service FEIS, after Miller 2006) states the two species the other way round, without a viewpoint; it stands alone. |
| RHS ultimate-height envelope | RHS: 8–12 m; 10–20 years to ultimate size class. |
| Hardiness | H6 (RHS). |
| Flower / leaf / fruit season | Lilac to violet-blue flowers in late spring and early summer, with the leaves; pendulous racemes of 0.5–1 m and more; yellow leaves in autumn; velvety pods that hang on through winter. |
| Aspect and exposure | Full sun/partial shade; south/west sheltered sites, generous overhead clearance. See "Light, aspect and shelter". |
| Soil | Moist but well-drained; tolerates common garden soil types. |

**Evidence discipline:** height classes and mature size times are qualitative UK horticultural descriptors, **not** yearly extension, support-path reach or physical force values. This file makes no claim to cultivar-calibrated numerical growth rates.

## Species-specific biological logic

Woody main axes with flowering spurs and long racemes; prior-season flower-bud initiation means the plant must retain appropriate buds and laterals through winter. The racemes are borne at the ends of short leafy shoots (Bean), which is why this plant flowers with its leaves where *W. sinensis* flowers before them.

## Form: what the model draws

Every shape number below is in [`morphology.json`](morphology.json) with its source and evidence status; the drawing is `packages/viewer/src/plant/taxa/wisteria-floribunda-macrobotrys.ts`. Nothing in it is taken from the *Wisteria sinensis* models.

**Through the year, on a trained plant of six to ten years.**

- *Winter.* A bare framework: grey stems, the oldest fluted and rope-like where they have twined, with short spurs standing off them. A few brown pods still hang from last year's raceme stalks until they split, between mid January and mid March in the model.
- *Spring.* From early April the flower buds lengthen into slender tassels of dark violet buds; from mid April the leaves break, pale bronze and folded. By the second week of May the tassels are nearly full length and still shut.
- *Late spring into summer (15 May to late June in the model).* The racemes open from the base, which is the top of a hanging raceme, to the tip: open flowers above, buds below, and at the end bare stalk above with the last flowers at the bottom. 0.6 to 1.1 m long and 7 to 8 cm wide, about 150 flowers each, standing apart on stalks of 13 to 25 mm. Each flower is small beside that of *W. sinensis*: a pale violet standard about 12 mm long with a small yellow eye, over violet-blue wings and keel of 15 to 20 mm. The leaves are part open and still bronze-green when the first racemes open, and full grown before the last has gone.
- *Summer.* Dark green, glossy leaves 25 to 38 cm long, of 11 to 17 leaflets (most often 13 or 15): each leaflet about 7 cm by 2.2 cm, rounded at the base, broadest below the middle, drawn out to a long point, with a wavy edge. Long green shoots of the year are cut back in August. Where a raceme set, one to three flat grey-green velvety pods of 11 to 20 cm hang from what is left of its stalk.
- *Autumn.* The leaves turn clear yellow from early October and fall through November; the pods turn brown.

**Where the model simplifies.**

- One raceme model is scaled to each raceme's length, so a long raceme has larger flowers, not more of them (0.6 m: flowers at 0.7 of true size; 1.1 m: 1.3).
- The number of flowers to a raceme (150) is a choice: no count of this cultivar was found.
- Opening and petal fall each run down the raceme, but opening is over before petal fall starts. On the real plant the top is fading while the tip is still in bud; the model never shows both at once.
- A flower still shut is drawn as the open flower at three tenths of its size and darker. A real bud is about half as long as the flower and is held closer to the stalk. In bud, a raceme is drawn at a fifth of its length from the day growth starts and lengthens evenly until it opens; a real one starts as a scaly bud of a few centimetres and makes most of its length in its last two or three weeks, and goes on lengthening while it flowers.
- How many pods a raceme carries is not recorded. One, two or three are drawn, on a length of old stalk that is also a choice. Pods do not split and twist on the plant: a pod is there, whole, until its day comes, and then it is gone.
- Old bark is grey and fluted; the lenticels that dot it are not drawn. Down on young shoots, leaves and pods is colour and dullness only.
- Stipules, the small bracts under the flower buds, and the ten stamens inside the keel are not drawn.
- The leaf has one size range and one form: the smaller, fewer-leafleted leaves of the flowering spurs are the same model at nine tenths of the length, picked from the variants with fewer leaflets.

## Light, aspect and shelter

- **Light classes accepted.** Full sun or partial shade (RHS records of 'Kyushaku' and of the species). Full shade is not listed. The RHS growing guide: wisterias "do best" in full sun; they "will grow in slight shade but flowering will be reduced", with few if any flowers in deep shade. NC State Extension calls the species shade intolerant and asks for six hours or more of direct sun.
- **What the recommended aspect is for.** South- or west-facing (RHS). It is asked for *light and warmth*, and through them for flower: the RHS ties the aspect to full sun, and Useful Temperate Plants (after Bean and the RHS Dictionary) says that where summers are cool the plant needs the extra summer warmth of a sunny wall. Flower buds form the summer before (RHS), so it is last summer's sun that this spring's racemes depend on.
- **What shade does.** To growth, little that the sources record: the plant climbs towards the light and is at home on woodland edges (US Forest Service FEIS). To flowering, a great deal: reduced in slight shade, nearly none in deep shade (RHS); "too much shade" is one of the RHS's listed causes of a wisteria that does not flower.
- **Shelter.** The RHS lists the exposure as sheltered. Two reasons are given in the sources. Frost: spring frosts "can damage or distort developing flowers" and make the buds drop (RHS); Useful Temperate Plants adds shelter from cold winds and from early morning sun on frosty mornings, when frozen buds thaw too fast. This cultivar flowers later than *W. sinensis* and so meets fewer frosts in flower (a nursery, Havlis, makes the point), but its buds are lengthening through April and May. Wind: not stated in any source read; that metre-long racemes are battered in an exposed place is an inference and is not entered as a fact.
- **Too much sun or exposure.** No source read reports harm from sun in Britain. Drought does harm: dry soil from July to September, when next year's flower buds form, makes them fail (RHS), and a hot, dry wall foot is where that happens.
- **For a design.** Place it where the bearing wood has sun for most of the day in summer; do not rely on it for flower under other canopy or on a north side; keep the racemes out of frost pockets and cold draughts; and water at the root in late summer.

## What Bower must provide: local supports

Long, woody climbing routes along the glulam and its sub-lattice; leave vertical free space for racemes to hang without colliding with cross-members, lighting or visitors.

## Global structure placement logic

Upper shell/arch with visible underside; **prefer over *W. sinensis*** when very long suspended floral cascades are the essential design goal.

## Gardener intervention / pruning

Train and selectively thin; two-stage seasonal wisteria pruning, while protecting productive laterals. Inspect for attachment constriction. The RHS measure: in July or August the whippy growth of the year back to five or six leaves (about 30 cm), and in late winter the same shoots to two or three buds (about 10 cm).

## Shoots of the year

The long summer shoots are slender, almost unbranched and whippy; on a plant in full growth they make 0.6 to 3 m in a season (Ohio State University, of the genus). They are searching shoots: the tip circles until it meets something to wind round, and two that meet wind round each other. No source read gives how far one stands out before it bends, or the stiffness or density of the stems. By common observation, which is not a source, a free shoot arches over within a metre or so and then hangs or waves; it does not stand straight out into the air. The growth engine does not yet bend free shoots under their own weight or turn them to the light; that is being mended centrally, and this plant's shoot numbers and lengths have not been cut to hide it.

## Interactions with the broader palette

Pair with evergreen side zones and lower summer flowers, but avoid shading racemes with nearby climbers or stacking aggressive vines.

## Risks, exclusions and scientific caveats

Long racemes need clear height; harmful if eaten; mature woody mass and wind response not validated. The accepted name and its aliases are recorded in `taxon.json`. Old stems reach 10 cm through and, in very old plants, far more (38 cm is recorded): where they have twined round a member they bear on it as they thicken.

## Parameters to obtain or calibrate

Raceme-length distribution and vertical density; flowers to a raceme; how long one raceme stays in flower; pods to a raceme and the share of racemes that set; species-specific coiling, extension, spur/bud response, age-to-flowering and canopy light transmission; dates of leaf break, flowering and leaf fall in a design's own climate.

At minimum, bind all measured parameters to plant **age**, plant **part**, time step, site/climate, support properties and propagation/stock history. Keep missing values empty rather than extrapolating from species names or RHS maturity classes.
